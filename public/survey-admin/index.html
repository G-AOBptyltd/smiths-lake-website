How VillageFirst Surveys works

The big picture in one minute

VillageFirst Surveys has three faces that share one design. You build & manage here in the admin; residents respond on a public survey link; anyone can view results on a public results page.

1
Admin (this app) — create surveys, set who can respond, open/close them, watch results, manage villages. Sign-in required.
2
The survey link — a public page residents fill in (no login). Each survey has its own shareable link.
3
The results page — a public, charted results page (and a per-village "All results" hub). You control whether each survey's results are public.
Behind the scenes: survey settings live in Notion, responses land in a Google Sheet, and results are charted live — but you never touch any of that. It's all driven from this admin.
2

Create your first survey

New Survey → four quick steps
Start here

Click + New Survey in the sidebar. The builder walks you through four steps.

1
Pick a template — choose the question style that fits your goal (see section 3). This shapes the whole survey.
2
Details — name it, write a short purpose, set Opens / Closes dates, choose who can respond (the pills), and set Results visibility and a snapshot label for exports.
3
Questions — add your items/options. Each template gives you the right fields (rating items, choices, ranking lists, etc.).
4
Activate — review and publish. The system creates the response sheet and your shareable survey link automatically.
Tip: you can save and come back. A survey stays a draft until you activate it — drafts are never visible to the public.

4

Survey types in depth

Purpose · plain-English method · a worked example for each
Reference

Tap any type for what it's for, how it works in everyday terms, and a ready-to-copy example — the exact questions, the answer format, and why each question earns its place.

✅ Quick Poll — one fast decision
✓ Best for: a single yes/no or pick-one decision you need fast✕ Not for: nuanced topics or anything needing a "why"
Purpose

Settle a single question quickly and get a clear majority. Best for go / no-go decisions where you don't need detail.

Methodology (plain English)

Ask one closed question with a few options. Everyone picks one; you read off the percentages and the most-chosen option wins. Keeping it to one question means almost everyone finishes.

Example — "Foreshore kiosk hours"
Q1. Should the foreshore kiosk open year-round?
◯ Yes, year-round · ◯ No, summer only · ◯ Unsure
Why: one clear decision, mutually-exclusive options, and an "Unsure" option so nobody is forced to guess.
📋 Likert Agreement — measure attitudes
✓ Best for: measuring sentiment and finding where opinion splits✕ Not for: ranking many options or collecting open ideas
Purpose

Gauge how strongly people agree or disagree with a set of statements — and see where the community is united or divided.

Methodology (plain English)

List a few short statements. For each, people choose from Strongly disagree → Strongly agree (a 5-point scale). You report the share who agree and how opinions spread. Each statement should cover just one idea.

Example — "Village plan sentiment"
Q1. "The village needs more shade and seating."
Strongly disagree · Disagree · Neutral · Agree · Strongly agree
Why: a single, concrete statement so the score is unambiguous.
Q2. "Short-stay holiday rentals should be limited."
(same 5-point scale)
Why: a deliberately divisive item — the spread of answers shows exactly where the community splits.
⭐ Star / Rating Review — satisfaction with things
✓ Best for: post-event or per-service satisfaction✕ Not for: comparing trade-offs or choosing between concepts
Purpose

Measure satisfaction with specific items — an event, a service, facilities — and pinpoint what worked and what didn't.

Methodology (plain English)

List the items; people rate each 1–5 stars, with an optional comment. You compare the average rating per item to see highs and lows.

Example — "Australia Day event"
Q1. Live music
★ 1–5
Why: a per-item rating pinpoints the highlight.
Q2. Parking & access
★ 1–5
Why: separating logistics from content shows where to put next year's effort.
Q3. Any comments?
(open text)
Why: explains the "why" behind a low score.
💬 Open Feedback / Ideas — in their own words
✓ Best for: surfacing ideas and issues you didn't think to ask✕ Not for: anything you need to measure precisely or rank
Purpose

Gather ideas, concerns and suggestions freely — and discover things you didn't think to ask about.

Methodology (plain English)

Ask one or two open questions. Answers are read and grouped into themes, then counted, so common threads rise to the top. The platform auto-tags themes to help.

Example — "Have your say"
Q1. What one thing would most improve the village?
(open text)
Why: open-ended surfaces priorities you'd never have listed yourself.
Q2. Anything else you'd like the committee to know?
(open text)
Why: a catch-all that often holds the most useful comment.
🎯 Priority Ranking (MaxDiff) — true priorities from a long list
✓ Best for: prioritising a long list (8+ items)✕ Not for: short lists or when each item needs an absolute score
Purpose

Find the community's genuine top priorities from a long list — without everything ending up "very important".

Methodology (plain English)

People see small groups of items and pick the best and worst in each. Repeat a few times with different groups. A score is built from how often each item is chosen best versus worst. The forced best/worst choice is what makes the priorities real.

Example — "Recreation reserve priorities"
Q. Of these four, which is MOST and which is LEAST important?
Event space · Pump track · Sculpture garden · Off-leash dog area → pick one Most, one Least (repeats with new sets)
Why: forcing a most/least choice stops everyone rating all items "important" and produces an honest ranking.
🔢 Ranked Choice — full order of a short list
✓ Best for: fully ordering a short list (≤7 items)✕ Not for: long lists (too hard to order) or budget splits
Purpose

Get each person's complete order of preference for a short list (up to ~7 items).

Methodology (plain English)

People drag the items into order, 1st to last. You average everyone's positions to get the overall ranking. Best when the list is short enough to order in one go.

Example — "Foreshore upgrade options"
Q. Drag these into your order of preference:
Boat ramp & pontoon · Shaded picnic lawn · Accessible pathway · Extra parking · BBQ block
Why: a full per-person ranking is more precise than "pick your top 3" when the list is short.
💰 Budget Allocation — priorities under a fixed budget
✓ Best for: spending/funding trade-offs and grant evidence✕ Not for: yes/no decisions or measuring attitudes
Purpose

Reveal real spending priorities when money is limited — strong, defensible evidence for grant applications.

Methodology (plain English)

Give everyone a fixed pot (say 100 points, or $100k) to split across the options. It must add up to the total, so people make genuine trade-offs. You report the average split.

Example — "$100k community grant"
Q. Distribute 100 points across these (must total 100):
Walking tracks ___ · Playground ___ · Events fund ___ · Tree planting ___ · Signage ___
Why: the fixed total forces trade-offs — "a bit of everything" isn't possible, so the real priorities show.
📈 Importance vs Performance — what to fix first
✓ Best for: deciding what to improve first across services✕ Not for: choosing between options or quick one-off polls
Purpose

Decide where to act first by comparing how important something is against how well it's currently done.

Methodology (plain English)

For each service, ask two ratings: how important is it, and how well is it performing now (1–5 each). Items that are high-importance but low-performance are your priorities — the results plot onto a simple four-box "focus here" quadrant.

Example — "Village services"
Q1a. Roads & verges — how important to you?
1–5
Q1b. Roads & verges — how well done right now?
1–5
Why: the gap between importance and performance is the action signal — a big gap means fix it first.
⚖️ Design Option Preference (Conjoint) — choose between concepts
✓ Best for: choosing between 2 concepts and finding the real driver✕ Not for: quick questions or long lists (higher respondent effort)
Purpose

Choose between design concepts and understand what actually drives the choice — not just what people say matters.

Methodology (plain English)

People rate the features both designs share, rate how important the few key differences are, then make a series of package choices where those features are varied. Comparing the choices reveals which feature truly swings the decision. A final question captures the overall favourite.

Example — "Village centre design"
Part A. Rate the shared features (parking, seating, planting…)
1–5 each
Why: confirms the common ground both designs get right.
Part C. "Which package do you prefer?" — across several pairs where features change
pick A or B each time
Why: the revealed trade-offs expose the real driver — e.g. people keep the extra parking unless a pedestrian crossing is added.
Part E. Overall, which design do you prefer?
Option 1 · Option 2 · Hybrid
Why: a single headline preference for the council submission.
📊 Annual Satisfaction — yearly community pulse
✓ Best for: a repeatable yearly pulse with an NPS trend✕ Not for: a single decision or deep prioritisation
Purpose

A yearly health check of community sentiment you can track over time.

Methodology (plain English)

Rate a set of services 1–5, ask the "recommend" question (0–10) to produce an NPS advocacy score, and leave room for open comments. Run it the same way each year to see the trend.

Example — "Annual community pulse"
Q1. Rate each: environment, events, tracks, roads, parking…
1–5
Why: service-level detail on what's working.
Q2. How likely are you to recommend living here to a friend?
0–10
Why: the standard NPS question — one number that summarises overall advocacy and is easy to track year on year.
Q3. What should we focus on next year?
(open text)
Why: turns the score into direction.
👥 Demographic / Registration — who responded
✓ Best for: profiling respondents or capturing membership✕ Not for: measuring opinions (pair it with another type)
Purpose

Understand who responded (to check the sample is representative) or capture member/registration details.

Methodology (plain English)

Ask a few structured profile questions — pick-lists for things you'll group by, open fields for details. You report counts per group and can split your other results by them.

Example — "Membership snapshot"
Q1. Age group
Under 35 · 35–54 · 55–69 · 70+
Why: lets you check all ages are represented and segment the findings.
Q2. Years in the village
<2 · 2–5 · 6–15 · 15+
Why: newcomers and long-timers often want different things.
Q3. Household type
Permanent · Holiday home · Renting
Why: a key VillageFirst segment for interpreting results.
🗂️ Multi-section — combine several types
✓ Best for: a consultation needing several styles in one✕ Not for: quick surveys (extra length cuts response rates)
Purpose

When one survey needs more than one style — for example some ratings, a priority exercise, and an open comment.

Methodology (plain English)

Build several of the simple types above as sections within one flow; each section keeps its own analysis. Use sparingly — longer surveys lose responses.

Example — "Village plan consultation"
Section 1 — Likert
attitudes to the draft plan
Section 2 — Budget allocation
split the funding
Section 3 — Open feedback
final ideas
Why: captures attitude, priorities and ideas in one sitting, while each section is still analysed cleanly.
Rule of thumb: match the method to the decision — a single decision → Quick Poll; sentiment → Likert; many priorities → MaxDiff or Budget Allocation; what-to-fix → Importance vs Performance; choosing a concept → Conjoint; ideas → Open Feedback.

Manage your surveys

The Dashboard is mission control

Every survey appears on the Dashboard with quick actions on its row.

Edit

Change safe fields any time (title, purpose, dates, visibility). Question changes after responses arrive prompt a safe duplicate-and-relaunch.

Duplicate

Clone a survey to reuse its structure for a new round or another village.

Survey link / Open

Copy the shareable link or open the live survey to preview it.

Results →

Jump straight to that survey's charted results.

Status

Set draft → live → paused → closed from the row's dropdown. Paused stops new responses; closed locks it.

Lifecycle & scheduling

Open dates, close dates, pause & reopen
1
Opens / Closes dates — set in the builder. A survey scheduled for the future won't accept responses until its open date; it auto-closes after the close date.
2
Pause any time to temporarily stop responses without closing — handy mid-campaign.
3
Reopen a paused survey by setting it back to live.
Statuses at a glance: draft (hidden, editable) · scheduled (waiting for open date) · live (accepting responses) · paused (temporarily stopped) · closed (finished).

Visibility & privacy

Who can see the results
Important

Each survey has a Results visibility setting. Choose it in the builder, or flip it instantly with the Results public toggle on each card in Results.

Public

Live results visible to anyone with the link, and listed on the public hub.

Committee only

Hidden from the public hub and public page — visible to you in the admin. Use this for test or internal surveys.

Public after close

Results stay private until the survey closes, then publish automatically.

Respondent after completion

Each person sees results right after they submit — nice for engagement.
Hide a test survey fast: go to Results, find its card, and switch Results public to Off. It disappears from the public hub immediately but stays in your admin.

Results & sharing

From live charts to a council-ready page

Open Results to see every survey as a card with its live response count. Click one for the full charted page — the same page the public sees.

1
Rich visuals — headline takeaway, KPI cards, donuts, ranked bars, 1–5 distributions, NPS gauge, importance×performance quadrant — auto-matched to the template.
2
Tabs & navigation — bigger surveys split into tabs; a breadcrumb, survey switcher and prev/next let you move between surveys.
3
Share it — View Public → opens the resident-facing copy; the public All results hub lists every public survey in the village.
4
Export — Export CSV for the raw data, or Print / Save PDF for a clean snapshot (use the snapshot label for council submissions). A greyscale toggle helps printing.
Theming: results automatically use each village's colours, so a Smiths Lake page and a Blueys Beach page feel like their own place while sharing one design.

Comments & moderation

Open feedback, safely
1
Enable comments on a survey to collect open-text feedback alongside the structured questions.
2
Turn on moderation (per survey) if you want to review before the public sees comments.
3
Hide / show individual comments from the admin results view — a "🛡 Moderate comments" panel appears under the charts. Hidden comments never show publicly.
Always anonymous: public comments are shown without names or identifying details.

Working across villages

One platform, many communities
1
Switch village with the picker in the top bar — everything (dashboard, results, surveys) scopes to that village.
2
All Villages (super-admin) shows a rollup: per-village active surveys, totals, responses and participation, plus lifecycle controls.
3
Add a village with + Village. Lifecycle: Live → Suspend (pauses all its surveys) → Archive (also removes public access) → Delete (archived & empty only, with typed confirmation).

Best practice

Get more, better responses

Keep it short

5 focused questions beat 20 vague ones. Respect people's time and completion rates climb.

Lead with purpose

A one-line "why this matters" at the top lifts both starts and quality.

Pick the right respondents

Only offer the respondent pills that apply — it segments your results cleanly.

Share widely

Post the survey link in the newsletter, socials and noticeboards. Add the QR code for in-person events.

Snapshot before you submit

Set a snapshot label and Print/Save PDF for a dated record to attach to council submissions.

Close the loop

Share results back with the community — it builds trust and boosts the next survey.
13

Troubleshooting

Quick answers to common questions
My survey isn't showing on the public results hub

Check its Results visibility. Only Public surveys appear on the hub; committee-only and draft surveys are hidden. Flip the Results public toggle on the survey's card in Results.

The results say "No responses yet"

That's the empty state — results appear as soon as the first response lands. Confirm the survey is live and within its open/close dates, and that you've shared the survey link.

I can't change the questions after responses came in

To keep the data valid, structural changes after responses use duplicate-and-relaunch (a new version). Safe fields — title, purpose, dates, visibility — stay editable any time.

Counts look different between the hub and a survey's page

The hub shows live counts; an individual page reads the full sheet. They reconcile within moments. If a number looks stuck, reload the page.

How do I make results private again?

Set Results public to Off (committee-only) on the survey's card, or change visibility in the builder. It hides immediately from the public.

Who do I contact for help?

Reach AOB at support@agilityops.com.au for account access, new villages, or anything this playbook doesn't cover.
